Hospital Denials: The Claim That Comes Back

A denied claim is not usually a lost claim. Most denied claims are eventually paid, after staff time, appeals, and delay. The cost sits in that gap: an initial denial rate near 12% against a final denial rate under 3%, with tens of billions of dollars leaking out of hospital revenue in between. This page tracks both ends of that gap, because either number alone misleads.

Methodology

Included: Kodiak Solutions revenue-cycle benchmarking (the largest hospital sample with a published 2024–2025 comparison), American Hospital Association published denial figures, and the HHS Office of Inspector General's 2022 audit of Medicare Advantage denials. Excluded: vendor marketing claims without a stated hospital sample; payer-specific denial rates for named insurers, which Kodiak reports in aggregate but not as verified public tables this session; and Change Healthcare denial data, for which no verified public figure was found this session. OIG figures describe a June 2019 sample and are not a current denial rate.

What the numbers mean

Read the two Kodiak numbers together and the story changes. An 11.6% initial denial rate sounds like a billing failure; a 2.7% final rate sounds like a solved problem. The truth is the work between them: appeals, rework, and delayed cash, priced by the AHA at $19.7 billion a year in provider cost, with leakage rising 25% in a single year to more than $48 billion. The OIG audit adds the sharper edge — in its sample, 13% of Medicare Advantage prior-authorization denials and 18% of payment denials should not have happened under Medicare's own rules. Denials are not only an administrative tax; in a measurable share of cases, they are wrong on the merits.
