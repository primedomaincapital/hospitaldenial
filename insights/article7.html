How Long Does It Take to Appeal a Denied Hospital Claim?

8 MIN READ · HOSPITALDENIAL.COM · NOVEMBER 2026

The denial letter arrived on a Thursday, and the clock started before anyone in the hospital read it. The claim was for a knee replacement, the payer was a Medicare Advantage plan, and the reason was medical necessity — the same reason stamped on a third of the hospital's denials that quarter. The revenue cycle manager did the math in her head: the plan allows 60 days to file the first appeal, the decision takes another 60, and if that fails, the next level allows 180 days to file and 60 more to decide. Best case, the money comes back in four months. Realistic case, closer to a year. The patient had already gone home. The surgeon had already been paid. Only the appeal was still on the schedule, and it would be there for a long time.

Five levels, five clocks

For traditional Medicare, the appeal process is a formal ladder with five rungs, and each rung has two deadlines: one to file, one to decide. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services publishes the whole map. Level one is redetermination by the Medicare Administrative Contractor — the company that processed the claim in the first place, reviewed by staff who had nothing to do with the original decision. The hospital has 120 days from receipt of the initial determination to file, and the contractor has 60 days to decide. Level two is reconsideration by a Qualified Independent Contractor, an outside reviewer that can bring in a panel of physicians. The filing window stretches to 180 days; the decision clock stays at 60. If the contractor misses its deadline, the hospital can escalate straight to level three.

Level three is where the process leaves the contractor system entirely: a hearing before an administrative law judge at the Office of Medicare Hearings and Appeals. The filing window tightens to 60 days, the decision window to 90, and the claim must meet an amount-in-controversy threshold of $200 for calendar year 2026. Level four is review by the Medicare Appeals Council, part of the Departmental Appeals Board at Health and Human Services — 60 days to file, 90 days to decide. Level five is judicial review in federal district court, with a 2026 threshold of $1,960 in controversy and 60 days to file. Add the filing windows and the decision clocks together, and a claim that runs the full course can take well over a year from denial to final answer — and that assumes every deadline is met on both sides.

The commercial clock runs faster — and harsher

Commercial and Medicare Advantage denials run on different rules, and the deadlines are shorter. Under the Affordable Care Act, an insurer must decide an urgent prior authorization request within 72 hours and a standard request within seven calendar days, and must give a specific reason for every denial — requirements that took operational effect on January 1, 2026 under the CMS Interoperability and Prior Authorization final rule, CMS-0057-F. When a denial lands, the appeal windows are measured in days, not months: urgent appeals within 72 hours, pre-service appeals within 30 days, post-service appeals within 60 days. External review — the independent reviewer who steps in after the plan's internal process is exhausted — generally has 45 days to decide a standard case.

The volume behind those deadlines is enormous. KFF's August 2026 analysis of CMS data found that Medicare Advantage organizations denied 12 percent of prior authorization requests on average, Medicaid managed care plans denied 14 percent, and Affordable Care Act marketplace plans denied 18 percent. Within Medicare Advantage, the spread between plans was wide: UnitedHealth denied 17 percent of prior authorization requests, the highest among large insurers, while Elevance denied 5 percent, the lowest. The plans also differ in what happens next. KFF found that 67 percent of appealed Medicare Advantage denials were overturned — two out of three challenges succeed, when they are filed at all.

Post-acute care: the longest waits in the system

Nowhere is the timeline longer than in post-acute care. The Department of Health and Human Services Office of Inspector General reported in June 2026 that prior authorization denial rates for post-acute services ran far above the program average: for long-term care hospitals, CVS/Aetna denied 80 percent of requests, Humana 72 percent, and UnitedHealth 71 percent. For inpatient rehabilitation facilities, UnitedHealth denied 66 percent, Humana 54 percent, and CVS/Aetna 51 percent. The appeals told a different story: 36 percent of appealed long-term care hospital denials were overturned, and 43 percent of inpatient rehabilitation denials. A patient waiting for a rehabilitation bed while the authorization cycles through three levels of review is not waiting on medicine. They are waiting on paperwork, in a hospital bed that costs thousands of dollars a day.

The aggregate numbers confirm the pattern. Experian Health's 2026 State of Claims survey of 204 healthcare professionals found 75 percent saying denials are increasing, and 40 percent saying denial rates exceed 10 percent of claims. The HFMA considers 5 to 10 percent the acceptable benchmark. Kodiak Solutions reported that initial denials fell to 10.63 percent in the first half of 2026 — but final denials rose 9.1 percent, and commercial takebacks rose 12 percent. The denial is not getting faster. It is getting later.

The counterargument: deadlines protect both sides

The long timelines are not an accident, and they are not entirely the payers' doing. The five-level Medicare structure exists because each level is supposed to catch a different kind of error: the contractor catches the coding mistake, the independent reviewer catches the clinical disagreement, the judge catches the legal dispute. A system that resolved every denial in a week would resolve most of them wrong. The filing deadlines also protect the process — a claim appealed two years after the fact is a claim whose records are cold, whose physicians have moved on, and whose facts can no longer be reconstructed.

Hospitals contribute to their own delays, too. Appeals filed late, appeals filed without the operative note, appeals filed to the wrong address — every one of them restarts the clock or kills the claim outright. About 65 percent of denied claims are never reworked at all, according to HFMA and MGMA data, which means the longest timeline in the system is the one that never starts. The process is slow. The bigger problem is how often it is never used.

What this means for revenue cycle teams

Run the appeal like a docket, not a queue. Every denial should carry its filing deadline, its decision clock, and its escalation date from the day it arrives — because a missed deadline is a lost claim, and the deadlines are measured in days for commercial payers and weeks for Medicare. Track the overturn rate by level: if level-one redeterminations are succeeding at a high rate, the problem is documentation, not medical necessity, and the fix belongs at the front end. If cases are reaching the administrative law judge stage, the problem is the payer's clinical criteria, and the fix belongs in the contract negotiation.

And file early. The 67 percent overturn rate on appealed Medicare Advantage denials and the 80.7 percent overturn rate on appealed denials generally — KFF's analysis of CMS data — both measure the same thing: denials that get challenged usually do not survive. The appeal is not a courtesy. It is the most reliable revenue recovery tool a hospital owns, and the only cost of using it is the calendar discipline to file before the window closes.
