Denial Rates by Payer: Which Insurers Deny Most in 2026

8 MIN READ · HOSPITALDENIAL.COM · NOVEMBER 2026

The revenue cycle director keeps a spreadsheet that no one outside her department ever sees. It has one row per payer and one column per quarter, and the numbers in it decide which claims get appealed first, which get appealed at all, and which get written off without a fight. The top of the list never changes. The same three names sit there every quarter, in the same order, with denial rates two to four times higher than the plans at the bottom. She does not need a study to tell her which insurers deny most. She needs the study to prove it to the people who negotiate the contracts — and in 2026, the data finally exists to do that.

The payer spread, in one table

The cleanest comparison comes from KFF's August 2026 analysis of CMS data on prior authorization requests. Medicare Advantage organizations denied 12 percent of prior authorization requests on average. Medicaid managed care plans denied 14 percent. Affordable Care Act marketplace plans denied 18 percent. Those are program-level averages, and they already tell a story: the more a plan manages care through prior authorization, the more it denies. But the averages hide the spread that matters. Within Medicare Advantage, UnitedHealth denied 17 percent of prior authorization requests — the highest rate among large insurers — while Elevance denied 5 percent, the lowest. The same program, the same rules, the same patient population: a threefold difference between the plan at the top of the table and the plan at the bottom.

CMS's own disclosures for calendar year 2025 tell the same story at finer grain. The national mean prior authorization denial rate across Medicare Advantage plans was about 11.5 percent, but standard denial rates ranged from under 2 percent to above 27 percent depending on the plan, and some individual plans denied more than half of all requests. Averages in this market are nearly useless. The plan is the unit that matters, and the worst plans deny at rates ten times the best.

Denial rates by payer type

The spread widens further when the comparison moves from prior authorization to all claims. Industry benchmarking for 2026 puts traditional Medicare at about 5 percent — the program's fee-for-service structure has no profit motive to deny, and its denial rate is the floor the market is measured against. Medicare Advantage runs about 15.7 percent, roughly three times traditional Medicare. Commercial payers run about 21 percent. Medicaid inpatient claims run roughly 44 percent — nearly half of all inpatient claims denied at first pass, a figure that reflects both the complexity of the Medicaid population and the administrative burden of the program's documentation requirements.

The trend is moving the wrong way for the plans that already deny most. The American Hospital Association's September 2024 report found care denials rose an average of 20.2 percent for commercial claims and 55.7 percent for Medicare Advantage claims between 2022 and 2023. Experian Health's 2026 State of Claims survey of 204 healthcare professionals found 75 percent saying denials are increasing, and 40 percent saying denial rates exceed 10 percent of claims. CareCloud's 2026 State of Hospital Denials white paper found 41 percent of providers now run denial rates above 10 percent, up from 30 percent in 2022. The HFMA considers 5 to 10 percent the acceptable benchmark. The market as a whole has blown past it.

Post-acute care: where the gap becomes a chasm

The starkest payer differences show up in post-acute care, where the authorization decision determines whether a patient leaves the hospital at all. The Department of Health and Human Services Office of Inspector General reported in June 2026 that prior authorization denial rates for long-term care hospitals reached 80 percent at CVS/Aetna, 72 percent at Humana, and 71 percent at UnitedHealth. For inpatient rehabilitation facilities, UnitedHealth denied 66 percent, Humana 54 percent, and CVS/Aetna 51 percent. These are not marginal differences in clinical judgment. They are different companies running different businesses under the same program rules.

The appeals data sharpens the point. When providers appealed, 36 percent of long-term care hospital denials were overturned, and 43 percent of inpatient rehabilitation denials. KFF found that 67 percent of appealed Medicare Advantage denials were overturned overall, and 80.7 percent of appealed denials were overturned across all payers, according to its analysis of CMS data. A denial rate that high, combined with an overturn rate that high, describes a system that denies first and asks questions only when challenged — and most denials are never challenged. About 65 percent of denied claims are never reworked, according to HFMA and MGMA data.

The counterargument: case mix is not a conspiracy

Before naming the worst offenders, the honest caveat: denial rates are not purely a measure of payer behavior. They reflect case mix. Medicaid inpatient claims carry the sickest patients, the most complex social circumstances, and the thinnest documentation — all of which produce legitimate denials at higher rates. Medicare Advantage plans that manage older, sicker populations will deny more than plans with healthier members. A plan that authorizes aggressively and audits later will show a low initial denial rate and a high takeback rate instead. Kodiak Solutions' mid-2026 data showed exactly that pattern: initial denials fell to 10.63 percent in the first half of 2026, while final denials rose 9.1 percent and commercial takebacks rose 12 percent. The denial moved, it did not disappear.

None of that explains a threefold spread between two Medicare Advantage plans covering similar populations, or a plan denying 80 percent of long-term care hospital authorizations while its competitors deny 71 percent. Case mix explains some of the gap. It does not explain all of it. The rest is policy — and policy is a choice.

What this means for hospitals

Build the payer scorecard and use it. Denial rate, overturn rate, time to decision, and appeal success rate, by payer and by service line, updated quarterly. The data exists now in a way it did not three years ago: KFF's plan-level analysis, CMS's annual disclosures, the OIG's post-acute review. A hospital that walks into a contract negotiation with its own numbers — this plan denies 17 percent of our authorizations and overturns two-thirds of them on appeal — is negotiating from evidence, not anecdote.

Second, staff the appeal to the payer, not to the average. A denial from a plan with a 5 percent denial rate is probably a documentation problem, and the fix is at the front end. A denial from a plan with a 17 percent denial rate and a 67 percent overturn rate is probably a policy problem, and the fix is a systematic appeal program with physician-advisor review. The same denial, from two different payers, deserves two different responses.

Third, treat the spread as a contracting issue. A payer that denies at three times the rate of its competitors is not a clinical partner. It is a cost center. The hospitals that price that cost into their negotiations — with data, quarter after quarter — are the ones that will see the numbers move. The ones that do not will keep funding the gap, one denied claim at a time.

Fourth, use the scorecard in the places that matter beyond the contract. A payer that denies 17 percent of authorizations and overturns two-thirds of them on appeal is a payer whose clinical criteria deserve a challenge through the plan's own peer-to-peer process, through the state insurance commissioner's complaint process, and through the hospital association's payer-performance reporting. The data that wins a contract negotiation is the same data that wins a regulatory complaint — and the hospitals that keep it systematically are the ones whose complaints get answered.
