What Does a Denied Claim Cost a Hospital?

8 MIN READ · HOSPITALDENIAL.COM · NOVEMBER 2026

The email arrived on a Tuesday in February, addressed to the chief financial officer of a 300-bed community hospital in Ohio. A single claim, $41,200, for an inpatient stay that had already happened, a patient already discharged, a bed already turned over. The payer's verdict: the admission did not meet medical necessity. The hospital's revenue cycle team knew the chart was thin — a physician note written at 2 a.m., a severity score never entered — and they knew what came next. A coder would pull the record. A nurse reviewer would draft the appeal. A physician advisor would sign it. Six weeks later, the money might come back. The cost of the rescue, before a single dollar of the claim itself was recovered, runs about $103 in staff time, according to the Healthcare Financial Management Association's fall 2025 survey. A clean claim, by contrast, costs about $6.50 to process. That sixteen-fold gap is the whole denial business in miniature.

The $262 billion headline — and what it actually measures

Start with the scale. The most quoted number in the denial business is nearly a decade old, and it still frames every board conversation. A 2017 analysis by Change Healthcare, built on 2016 claims data from 724 hospitals and more than 3.3 billion transactions, found that roughly 9 percent of the $3 trillion in hospital claims submitted each year were initially denied — about $262 billion in charges. For a typical health system, as much as 3.3 percent of net patient revenue sat at risk, an average of $4.9 million per hospital. About 63 percent of those denied claims were recoverable. Getting them back cost roughly $118 per claim, or as much as $8.6 billion a year in appeals-related administrative work across the industry. The headline number is dated. The mechanics it describes are not.

The rework cost is the first of three bills a denial sends, and the most visible, because it shows up as headcount. Premier's data puts the cost of contesting a single hospital denial at $57.23 in 2023, up from $43.84 the year before — a 30 percent jump in twelve months, driven by the clinical review and physician-advisor time that simple billing fixes never needed. Industry estimates put the total at roughly $19.7 billion a year spent just on the administrative work of contesting denials: chart review, documentation assembly, appeal drafting, payer follow-up. That is money spent arguing about care already delivered, by people who could otherwise be doing almost anything else.

The money that never comes back

The second bill is the money that never comes back at all. About 65 percent of denied claims are never reworked, according to HFMA and MGMA data — abandoned because the dollar value is too small, the documentation too thin, or the staff too stretched to chase it. Kodiak Solutions, whose revenue-cycle data covers more than 2,300 hospitals and 350,000 physicians, puts the result in plain terms: providers lost more than $48 billion in net revenue in 2025 to final claim denials and uncollected patient balances, up from $38.6 billion in 2024. The median final denial rate rose from 2.5 percent to 2.7 percent in the same period. CareCloud's 2026 State of Hospital Denials white paper adds the distribution: 41 percent of providers now run denial rates above 10 percent, up from 30 percent in 2022.

The third bill is the one no general ledger captures cleanly: time. A denied claim sits in accounts receivable for weeks or months while the hospital has already paid the nurses, the drugs, the electricity. The American Hospital Association's September 2024 report found administrative costs now account for more than 40 percent of the total expenses hospitals incur delivering care, with care denials rising an average of 20.2 percent for commercial claims and 55.7 percent for Medicare Advantage claims between 2022 and 2023. Every denied claim is a small loan the hospital makes to the payer, interest-free, for the duration of the appeal. At scale, that is a working-capital problem, not a billing problem.

Most of it is self-inflicted

The most galling number in the denial business is not the size of the bill but how much of it is self-inflicted. Becker's Hospital Review puts the share of denials that are avoidable at roughly 86 percent — registration errors, eligibility mistakes, authorization lapses, coding slips. The HFMA's 2024 CFO survey found 82 percent of health system CFOs believe payer denials have increased significantly since before the pandemic, and 90 percent cite denials as their top revenue cycle challenge. The industry is not losing a fair fight. It is losing a fight it mostly starts itself, then paying $103 a claim to finish.

There is a human cost buried in the same numbers. A 2025 survey by the American Journal of Managed Care found that 89 percent of clinicians report prior authorization requirements contribute to burnout. The physician advisor reviewing denials at midnight is the same physician who was supposed to be seeing patients at noon. The denial business does not just consume money. It consumes the scarcest resource a hospital owns: clinical attention.

The counterargument: some of this cost is the system working

The payers have an answer, and it is not entirely wrong. Denials exist because improper payments exist. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services identifies insufficient documentation as the single leading root cause of improper payments nationally — reviewers cannot confirm a payment was proper because the supporting documentation was not there. A denial that forces a hospital to produce the record is, in that light, the system working as designed. And the $262 billion figure deserves a discount of its own: it counts billed charges, not collectible revenue, and hospitals routinely collect a fraction of charges. The true economic loss is smaller than the headline.

That argument has limits. A denial that arrives on a claim the payer already paid, for care it already authorized, is not a documentation problem. And the asymmetry is the point: the payer holds the money while the hospital appeals, and most denials are never challenged at all. The 80.7 percent overturn rate on appealed denials — KFF's analysis of CMS data — measures only the denials hospitals had the resources to fight. The denials that cost the most are the ones nobody ever contests.

What this means for a hospital's budget

First, price the denial, not just the claim. Most revenue cycle dashboards track the denial rate and stop there. The number that matters is the cost per denial worked — staff hours, advisor fees, and the revenue abandoned when a claim is never reworked — set against the recovery rate by payer and denial reason. A hospital that knows its cost per denial can decide, claim by claim, which fights are worth having.

Second, the arithmetic favors prevention by a wide margin. A clean claim costs about $6.50 to process; a reworked denial costs about $103. Every dollar spent on front-end accuracy — eligibility checks, prior authorization, documentation at the time of care — returns a multiple of itself in appeals avoided. The 86 percent of denials that are avoidable are the cheapest revenue a hospital will ever find.

Third, treat the appeal rate as a performance metric, not a headcount problem. If two-thirds of denials are never challenged, the cheapest revenue a hospital owns is the denial it already has the documentation to beat. The $262 billion figure will be quoted for years. The hospitals that survive it will be the ones that stopped treating denials as a billing nuisance and started treating them as a cost center with a measurable return.
