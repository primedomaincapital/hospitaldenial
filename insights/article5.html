The Clawback Shift: Payers Deny Less Up Front and Take More Later

6 MIN READ · HOSPITALDENIAL.COM · NOVEMBER 2026

The denial arrived eleven months after the hospital had already been paid. The claim was for an inpatient stay, the payer had processed it, the money had landed — and then the audit letter came, the payment was clawed back, and the revenue cycle team was told to appeal a decision about care delivered nearly a year earlier, with the documentation scattered across three systems and the physician who could explain it long gone. This is the denial trend that defines 2026, and it is not the one hospitals spent the last three years preparing for. Payers are denying less up front and taking more later. The fight has moved from the front door to the back door, and most hospitals have not noticed.

The numbers behind the shift

Kodiak Solutions' State of the Healthcare Revenue Cycle report, published in March 2026 and built on aggregated data from more than 2,300 hospitals and 350,000 physicians, puts the scale in plain terms: providers lost more than $48 billion in net revenue in 2025 to final claim denials and uncollected patient balances, up from $38.6 billion in 2024. The median final denial rate rose from 2.5 percent in 2024 to 2.7 percent in 2025. The median initial denial rate reached 11.6 percent in 2025, up from 11.4 percent in 2024. Then the mid-year data revealed the twist: in the first half of 2026, initial denials fell to 10.63 percent, from 12.00 percent in the first half of 2025 — but final denials rose 9.1 percent, and commercial takebacks rose 12 percent. Payers are denying less up front and clawing back more later.

The cost of fighting back is itself enormous. The American Hospital Association's Cost of Caring report puts hospitals' spending at an estimated $43 billion in 2025 trying to collect payments insurers already owed for care already delivered — including nearly $18 billion spent specifically on overturning claim denials. Experian Health's 2026 State of Claims survey of 204 healthcare professionals found 75 percent saying denials are increasing, and 40 percent saying denial rates exceed 10 percent of claims. The HFMA considers 5 to 10 percent the acceptable benchmark. Forty-one percent of providers now sit above 10 percent, up from 30 percent in 2022. By payer type, the spread is stark: traditional Medicare runs about 5 percent, Medicare Advantage about 15.7 percent, commercial payers about 21 percent, and Medicaid inpatient claims roughly 44 percent.

The rule that changed the front end

The front-end improvement has a cause: the CMS Interoperability and Prior Authorization final rule, CMS-0057-F, whose operational requirements generally began on January 1, 2026. Impacted payers — Medicare Advantage organizations, state Medicaid and CHIP programs, and Qualified Health Plan issuers on the Federally Facilitated Exchanges — must now send prior authorization decisions within 72 hours for urgent requests and seven calendar days for standard ones, give a specific reason for every denial, and publicly post prior authorization metrics each year, with the first set due by March 31, 2026. The API systems behind those requirements are due by January 1, 2027. The early data suggests the deadlines are biting: CMS's own CY2025 disclosures show a national mean prior authorization denial rate around 11.5 percent, with standard denial rates ranging from under 2 percent to above 27 percent depending on the plan, and some individual plans as high as 52 percent. Medicare Advantage insurers processed roughly 53 million prior authorization requests in 2024 and denied about 4.1 million of them — a 7.7 percent denial rate at massive scale.

And here is the number that should end any debate about whether most denials are legitimate: when providers appealed, 80.7 percent of denials were overturned, according to KFF's analysis of CMS data. Four out of five denials that get challenged do not survive the challenge. The American Medical Association's 2025 prior authorization survey found 32 percent of physicians saying requests are often or always denied, and 95 percent saying prior authorization delays access to necessary care.

The counterargument: the back door may be legitimate

Not every takeback is a payer overreach. Post-payment audits exist because improper payments exist, and CMS itself identifies insufficient documentation as the single leading root cause of improper payments nationally — reviewers cannot confirm a payment was proper because the supporting documentation was not there. Payers are also deploying natural language processing to compare clinical notes against submitted codes before a human ever sees the claim, and the January 2026 National Correct Coding Initiative bundling edit update — the largest single-cycle update since 2019, with a second wave in April 2026 — caught practices still running January's edit table. Some of the clawback growth is the system working as designed: catching claims that should never have been paid.

That argument has limits. A denial that arrives eleven months after payment, for care that was authorized and delivered, is not a documentation problem — it is a revenue strategy. And the asymmetry is the point: the payer holds the money while the hospital appeals, and most denials are never challenged at all. The 80.7 percent overturn rate measures only the denials hospitals had the resources to fight.

What this means for revenue cycle teams

The shift demands a different organization. Front-end denial prevention still matters — eligibility, benefits, prior authorization, documentation, coding — but it is no longer where the money is lost. The losses now arrive as takebacks, months after the fact, and defending them requires documentation that was complete at the time of care, audit-response capacity, and appeal rates far above today's. Three metrics deserve a place on every revenue cycle dashboard: the takeback rate by payer, the appeal rate on post-payment denials, and the overturn rate on appealed takebacks. A hospital that tracks only its initial denial rate is watching the front door while the money leaves through the back. The payers have already moved. The providers need to follow.
